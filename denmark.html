A CHRONOLOGY — FROM THE TUNDRA TO THE THRONE

Denmark, Post-Glacial

From reindeer hunters on post-glacial tundra to a monarchy still holding Arctic and Caribbean ground: the archaeology, the Viking diaspora, and five centuries of court, trade, and invention that follow.

c. 3,900 – 1,700 BCE
c. 3900–
2800 BCE

Funnelbeaker culture (TRB), the first farmers: Anatolian-derived ancestry replaces most hunter-gatherers, and long barrows, dolmens, passage graves, and the Sarup enclosures follow.

c. 3100–
2300 BCE

Pitted Ware coastal hunters overlap the late TRB.

c. 2850–
2250 BCE

Single Grave culture, Denmark's version of Corded Ware: individual graves under small barrows, battle-axes, amber, and crouched burials facing south, strongest in sandy Jutland. Its cord-impressed beaker pottery is typified by the Torsted Mølleby beaker (Himmerland, National Museum). Cattle-herding and barley cultivation drive forest clearance, and ancient DNA from sites like Gjerrild shows steppe/Yamnaya ancestry — a mix genetically closer to later Danes than either the Mesolithic population or early Funnelbeaker farmers were.

c. 2350–
1700 BCE

The Dagger Period: flint daggers (the Hindsgavl dagger) and Bell Beaker contact.

Bronze Age and Iron Age

c. 1,700 BCE – 800 CE
c. 1700–
500 BCE

Nordic Bronze Age: oak-coffin barrows, lurs, the Trundholm sun chariot, amber trade.

c. 500 BCE–

Pre-Roman through Germanic Iron Age: bog iron, Roman imports, the Gundestrup cauldron, gold hoards like Gallehus, and the first runes — laying down the regional elites behind the Viking Age.

Viking Age at home and abroad

c. 705 – 1035
c. 705

Ribe is already a market town.

c. 800–825

Norse settlement of the Faroe Islands (possibly preceded by Irish hermits), later passing from Norwegian to Danish–Norwegian to Danish control.

793

The Lindisfarne raid — the event that anchors the Viking Age in Western memory.

c. 808–810

Godfred confronts Charlemagne; the Danevirke is built up.

865–954

The Danelaw: the Great Heathen Army lands in 865 and takes York (866) and East Anglia. Alfred defeats Guthrum at Edington (878), and the Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum (c. 880–886) cedes Danish custom over eastern and northern England, including the Five Boroughs and Jórvík. English reconquest follows, ending with Eric Bloodaxe's expulsion from York in 954.

c. 870–930

Iceland: settlement (Landnám) around 871 ± 2, with the Althing traditionally founded in 930. It remains a commonwealth rather than a Danish colony, converts to Christianity around 999–1000, and passes to the Norwegian crown only in 1262–64.

911

Normandy: Charles the Simple grants Rollo land on the lower Seine (Treaty of Saint-Clair-sur-Epte). Rollo's own origin — Danish or Norwegian — is still debated. The duchy assimilates over generations; when William conquers England in 1066 it is a Norman undertaking, not Danish state policy, though part of the same diaspora.

c. 936–958

Gorm the Old raises the smaller Jelling stone for Thyra — the first written use of "Denmark" as a name.

c. 958–986

Harald Bluetooth unifies Denmark, claims Norway, and converts the Danes; the large Jelling stone and the Trelleborg-type ring forts date to his reign, and Jelling stands as the kingdom's conventional birth certificate (UNESCO-listed 1994).

c. 985–986

Greenland: Erik the Red leads Icelandic settlers to the Eastern Settlement (Brattahlíð / Qassiarsuk) and later the Western Settlement (near Nuuk), sustained by livestock, ivory, and church ties to Norway.

c. 1000–
1021

A Greenlandic–Icelandic base at L'Anse aux Meadows in Vinland/Newfoundland; timber felled there dates to AD 1021, but the occupation is brief and never a Danish royal colony.

991–1035

Sweyn Forkbeard takes England in 1013; his son Cnut the Great (1019–35) rules Denmark, England, and Norway together, though the English crown is lost after 1042. The Norman conquest of 1066 that follows is Norwegian and Norman in origin, not Danish.

High and late Middle Ages

1086 – 1523
1086

The murder of Canute IV (the Holy).

1104

Lund becomes the first Nordic archbishopric, at the time under Danish control.

1157–82

Valdemar I the Great ends the civil war and, with Absalon, takes Rügen and founds the castle at Copenhagen (1167).

1202–41

Valdemar II claims Estonia in 1219 (the origin of the Dannebrog legend), codifies the Jutland Law (1241), and sees the Baltic high-water mark end at Bornhöved (1227).

1282

The first håndfæstning.

1332–40

An interregnum leaves the realm mortgaged.

1340–75

Valdemar IV Atterdag buys the kingdom back, weathers the plague, and reclaims Skåne (1360).

1361–70

The Danish–Hanseatic War: Visby falls in 1361, Denmark wins at Helsingborg (1362), the Cologne Confederation forms, and Copenhagen is sacked (1368). The Peace of Stralsund (24 May 1370) grants the Hansa trade rights and a fifteen-year hold on Helsingborg, Skanør, Falsterbo, and Malmö.

1387–
1412

Margaret I builds the Kalmar Union (1397) of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden; the Faroes, Iceland, and the Greenland claim come along with Norway, while Orkney and Shetland are pledged to Scotland in 1468–69.

1420s

Eric of Pomerania builds Krogen at Helsingør to enforce the Sound Dues, collected until 1857.

1426–35

A second Dano–Hanseatic war.

1520–23

Christian II's reign ends with the Stockholm Bloodbath and Sweden's exit from the union; Denmark–Norway continues alone.

Reformation to absolutism

1534 – 1721
1534–36

The Count's Feud brings Christian III to power, and the Lutheran Reformation follows in 1536.

1559–88

Frederick II rebuilds Krogen as Kronborg (1574–85).

1560/
1600–20

Frederiksborg begins as Frederick II's hunting seat; his son Christian IV (born there in 1577, reigned 1588–1648) rebuilds it into Scandinavia's largest Dutch Renaissance palace at Hillerød, with the coronation chapel serving from 1671 to 1840.

c. 1600–01

Shakespeare sets Hamlet at Elsinore (Helsingør/Kronborg), drawing on Saxo's Amleth in the Gesta Danorum (c. 1200); the play has been staged at the castle since 1816, and the site was UNESCO-listed in 2000.

1629/
1658

Kronborg burns and is rebuilt, then falls to Sweden in the war that ends at Roskilde in 1658, costing Denmark Skåne, Halland, and Blekinge.

1660

Frederick III establishes hereditary absolutism.

1672–
1733

The Danish West Indies take shape: St. Thomas (1672), St. John (1718), and St. Croix (bought from France, 1733), built on sugar and enslaved labor, centered on Charlotte Amalie.

1700–21

The Great Northern War; the provinces lost stay lost.

1721

Hans Egede's mission marks the Danish recolonization of Greenland, where Norse farms now stand as ruins among living Inuit societies.

Enlightenment

1766 – 1803
1766–
1808

Christian VII, mentally ill, marries Caroline Matilda (sister of George III) in 1766.

1770–72

The period dramatized in A Royal Affair: the royal physician Johann Friedrich Struensee governs in the king's name, issuing Enlightenment-era decrees while conducting an affair with the queen, until Juliane Marie's coup on 17 January 1772. Struensee is executed on 28 April 1772; Caroline Matilda is banished and dies in 1775. (Film: En kongelig affære, 2012.)

1775

Royal Copenhagen porcelain is founded, sponsored by the same Juliane Marie who engineered the coup. Its mark uses three wavy lines — for the Storebælt, Lillebælt, and Øresund straits — and its first pattern is Blue Fluted. The king takes over the factory's finances in 1779, and it becomes the Royal Danish Porcelain Factory.

1790

The Flora Danica service, painted from the botanical atlas and originally intended for Catherine the Great (who dies before receiving it), is still hand-painted in Denmark today; it first appears in grand use at Christian VII's birthday in 1803.

19th century: losses, Golden Age

1801 – 1867
1801 &
1807

Britain attacks Copenhagen twice.

1814

The Treaty of Kiel cedes Norway to Sweden, while Iceland, the Faroes, and Greenland remain with Denmark.

1805–75

Hans Christian Andersen: his first fairy-tale booklet appears on 8 May 1835, followed by The Little Mermaid and The Emperor's New Clothes (1837), The Ugly Duckling (1843), The Snow Queen (1844), and The Little Match Girl (1845).

1847

J.C. Jacobsen founds Carlsberg on Valby hill, naming it for his son Carl; the Carlsberg Laboratory and Foundation follow in 1875–76, pure lager yeast is isolated in 1883, and the Foundation later funds both the rebuilding of Frederiksborg and much of Danish science.

1848–49

The March Revolution leads Frederick VII to sign the constitution, and slavery ends in the Danish West Indies that same year.

1853

Bing & Grøndahl porcelain is founded (mark: three towers), later merging with Royal Copenhagen in 1987.

1859

Frederiksborg burns and is rebuilt with Carlsberg money, becoming the Museum of National History in 1878.

1864

Christian IX takes the throne as Schleswig-Holstein is lost, leaving a small nation that still holds Arctic and Caribbean territories.

1867

A U.S. attempt to purchase St. Thomas and St. John fails.

20th and 21st centuries

1916 – 2024
1916–17

The Danish West Indies are sold to the United States for $25 million in gold, with the handover (Transfer Day) on 31 March 1917; in exchange, the U.S. recognizes Danish interests across all of Greenland. The islands become the U.S. Virgin Islands.

1918

Iceland becomes sovereign in personal union with Denmark, later a republic in 1944.

1920

North Schleswig is returned to Denmark; Christian X becomes the public face of the country during the later occupation years.

1923

August and Marie Krogh, with H.C. Hagedorn, found the Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium, the root of Novo Nordisk; it merges with Novo in 1989 after decades split between insulin and, later, GLP-1 drugs, backed by the Novo Nordisk Foundation.

1932–58

LEGO: Ole Kirk Christiansen starts a wooden-toy workshop in Billund during the Depression, names it LEGO ("leg godt") in 1934, moves into plastic, launches the System of Play in 1955, and patents the stud-and-tube brick on 28 January 1958. His son Godtfred succeeds him; Legoland Billund opens in 1968 and LEGO House in 2017.

1940–45

German occupation, punctuated by the ferrying of Danish Jews to Sweden in October 1943; the Faroes, Iceland, and Greenland remain under Allied control throughout.

1948/53/
79/09

Faroese home rule and Greenland's evolving status — first equal part of the realm, then home rule, then Self-Government — unfold in stages.

1973

Denmark joins the EEC/EU while keeping the krone; Greenland leaves the Communities in 1985.

1982

Per and Winnie Enevoldsen found Pandora jewellery in Copenhagen.

14 Jan
2024

Margrethe II abdicates after 52 years on the throne; Frederik X is proclaimed king, with Crown Prince Christian as heir.
